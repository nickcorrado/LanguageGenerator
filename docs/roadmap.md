# Roadmap

[examples/hollow.txt](../examples/hollow.txt), a short text adventure, is the test of what real text needs. The front end refuses 4 of its 190 sentences. Refusals are the easy part, though: a refusal names what's missing, while a sentence that glosses can still lose half of what it said. *The road from the coast washed out three winters ago* comes out as just 'the road from the coast collapsed', and *they are all smiling* loses its *all*. So this list counts both.

## Clauses

Roughly in the order to build them, since the later ones lean on the earlier ones.

- **More adverbs.** Words for where besides *here* and *there* (*inside*, *upstream*, *away*), which languages mostly make from spatial adpositions and relational nouns; *ago* and *later* after a noun (*three winters ago*, *a month later*); and a time first in the clause, as many languages put one, where today every adverb goes with the obliques, ranked by how near the verb it goes.
- **Places in noun phrases.** A noun phrase should be able to take a place, for *the old woman from the gate* and *a hunter at the fire*, which today only a clause can. The glosser now says them as relative clauses, 'a hunter who is at the fire', which keeps their meaning but has every language say them that way, where many would put the place in the noun phrase as English does, or make it an adjective, as Turkish *ateş-teki avcı* 'fire-LOC-ADJ hunter' does.
- **Roles English says with places.** English borrows spatial words for meanings that aren't places, and a language should borrow them only as its own map of roles says, as a time already shares the location's marker in most languages and not all. The ones the glosser now has to drop or bend:
  - Manner as a noun phrase (*in a line*, *in silence*). Russian puts it in the instrumental, *idti gus'kom* 'walk goose-INS' for 'in single file', Latin in the ablative, and others in an essive or a converb ('having become a line'), so it wants a marker with edges to the instrument, companion and location.
  - Opposition (*against the wind*, *fight against*), which German says with *gegen*, apart from *an* for touching. It takes a facing word or the goal's marker in many languages. The `against` configuration should then mean touching alone.
  - Having or wearing (*in armor*, *a man with a beard*), mostly marked like a companion, sometimes like a place, as Russian *v dospekhakh* is, and by a case of its own in Australian languages, like Warlpiri's proprietive *-kurlu*.
- **Experiencers.** Who feels or perceives something, as in *I like it*, *it seems to me* or *I'm cold*, which English says as a subject and many languages as a dative, as Spanish *me gusta* 'it pleases me' and German *mir ist kalt* 'to me is cold' do, the experiencer node of Haspelmath's map of dative functions. Bossong and Haspelmath find these datives across Europe and beyond it, though WALS doesn't count them. It's more than a role's marker, since the verb then agrees with what's liked rather than with who likes it, so it wants a class of verbs that take one.
- **Predicative possession.** *I have a book* is a verb for owning here in every language, where WALS 117A counts 'have' in only 63 of 240 languages. The rest say it as a place, 'at me is a book', as Russian *u menja kniga*, a genitive, 'my book is', a topic, 'as for me, a book is', or a companion, 'I am with a book', and the predicative possessor is a node of Haspelmath's dative map too, as Latin *est mihi liber* 'to me is a book' has it. A perfect made from 'have' would then lean on it (see More tense and aspect).
- **Existentials.** 'There is a dog in the house', which many languages say unlike 'the dog is in the house'.
- **Stacked operators.** The clause nests negations, situational modals and aspects in any order, but the realizer collects them onto its verb in fixed slots, one aspect, one modal and a negation inside or outside it, so *must be able to swim*, *has had to leave* and *has been singing* are refused, and an aspect or a negation is said the same on either side of what's next to it. An ordered list of what's on the verb, innermost first, folded into the verb complex, would say them all, with the language's marking deciding where each piece goes: in scope order, as the Mirror Principle has it, or in a fixed order of its own, as templatic languages have. The modals, aspects and negations below lean on it.
- **More modals.** A situational modal with `PRF` or `PROG`, mostly a regret, as *you should have gone* is, which is refused today though the realizer can say one. An epistemic verb said personally, as English *he must have left* is, which needs a way to keep the clause's tense on a bare verb, as English does with *have*, and epistemic 'should' and 'probably' told apart from 'must'. A modal on a command or a comparison. Languages whose negation is an affix could show its scope by where it goes, as Turkish *git-me-meli* 'must not go' puts it inside the modal and *gid-eme-z* 'cannot go' outside, and some languages put a modal's verb in a subjunctive, as Greek and the Balkan languages do, where today it's always bare.
- **More questions.** *Which* and *whose*; *how long*, *how often* and *how many*, which ask about a duration, a frequency and an amount; *who are you?*, with a question word as what *be* says; more than one question word, as in *who saw what?*; questions inside other clauses (*did anyone see what happened?*), which complement clauses make room for; alternative questions with *or*; and a yes-no marker in content questions too, as Japanese *ka* is, which WALS doesn't count. Question words are often built on one another, as Turkish *ne* 'what' is in *nere* 'where' and *ne zaman* 'what time, when', and often double as indefinites with no marker at all, as Mandarin *shénme* is both 'what' and 'something', where today an indefinite made from a question word always takes one.
- **More coordination.** Where the conjunction goes, which WALS doesn't count and is always between the two things here, though Latin *-que* follows the second; 'both … and' and 'either … or'; and coordinated addressees (*Marta and Ann, come*). Joined events, as in *she turns and walks away*, are each said as a clause at the shared tense; languages that chain clauses, as Turkic and Papuan ones do, would say the tense only on the last verb, many marking whether the next subject is the same, as switch reference does, and others serialize the verbs, as Yoruba and Mandarin do. A negation, a modal or a question over joined events, as in *you can't eat and drink here*, meaning not both, needs a way of saying it once, as *neither … nor* does, before it can be said.
- **More subordinate clauses.** A dependent clause whose time the clause it's in sets, as a purpose clause or 'to go' is, is said at the present where it keeps its verb; the main clause's time would be truer, as a sequence of tenses has it, or a subjunctive, as Greek *na* clauses and Romance *para que* ones have. Deranked copular clauses; *although*; the subject of a deranked clause in the genitive, as many languages put it; and a nominalizer on a deranked verb that takes a case, where the language has one.
- **More complement clauses.** Questions inside them, as in *did anyone see what happened?*, with 'whether' as 'if', as English and French *si* have it, or as the yes-no particle, as Russian *li*; a subjunctive on a balanced event's verb, as Greek *na* clauses and Romance *que* ones have; the subject of a nominalized complement in the genitive, as Turkish *sen-in gel-diğ-in-i* 'you-GEN come-NMLZ-2SG-ACC' has it; a clause as a subject (*that he came surprised her*); and indirect speech that shifts persons and tenses as each language does, which the glosser does the English way today, though many languages report speech only directly.
- **More relative clauses.** 'Whose', relativizing a possessor, which most languages do with a resumptive possessor or not at all; relative clauses on pronouns (*he who hesitates*) and with no noun at all, which the glosser now gives a general one; non-restrictive ones (*the miller, who was asleep*), which many languages set apart by intonation or not at all; a gap deeper than the relative clause's own roles (*the man that I said came*); and the types WALS 90A counts that keep the whole noun inside the clause: internally headed, as in Maricopa, correlative, as in Hindi, and adjoined, mostly in OV languages.
- **More comparison.** A negated comparison, one with a modal, or a command (*be more careful*), which a language comparing with two clauses would have to say in each; a standard shared by adjectives joined (*older and wiser than his brother*); a standard's clause said with its ellipsis, where today it's said whole, as 'than they started before' is for *than before*, and with be or a modal in one deranked clause, where today it's two clauses; a gap deeper than the standard's own clause (*taller than I thought he was*) and subcomparatives (*longer than it is wide*); a noun phrase's comparison said by a relative clause at its clause's time, where today it's the present; comparing amounts (*more bread*, *eats more*) and gradable adverbs (*sooner*, *more often*); similes with *be* (*she is like her mother*); and comparing with *less*, which many languages can only say by negating *more*. The [degree model](degree-model.md) has where each goes.
- **More degrees.** The [degree model](degree-model.md) has a place for each of these, and each wants a typology and a stage of its own:
  - Equatives (*as tall as*), which Haspelmath finds mark the property, the standard or both, very often with the similative's 'like' as the standard's marker. WALS doesn't count them.
  - Superlatives (*the tallest of the men*), with an affix or a word, whose form Bobaljik finds contains the comparative's, as Czech *nej-větší* 'most-bigger' does, or as a comparative with everything else as its standard, 'taller than all'.
  - 'Too', 'enough' and 'so … that', with the clause each takes, as in *too heavy to lift* and *so close that you could touch them*, which the glosser now says as 'very close'.
  - By how much, as in *a little bigger* and *two years older*, and a word of the language's own for the 'much' of *much bigger*, which is said with its 'very' today, as German says *viel größer* but *sehr groß*.
  - Bolinger's other degree words on a plain adjective: *rather*, *quite*, *a little*, *completely*.
- **Copula-like verbs.** *Grow*, *become*, *stay*, *seem*, and *look*, *sound*, *feel*, *smell* and *taste* said of what's perceived, which Hollow says with *be* now, as *you look terrible*, *your footsteps sound very loud*, *the moss grows thicker* and *it feels warmer than it should* are, losing the verb. Many languages say becoming with an inchoative instead, as Turkish *kalın-laş* 'thick-become' does, and Viberg's hierarchy of the senses says which of the lower senses share a higher one's verb. The [degree model](degree-model.md) has where they go.
- **More voice.** Antipassives, which ergative languages favor (WALS 108A), starting with the kind that demotes an indefinite object; reflexives; and causatives.
- **More tense and aspect.** More phases, as 'keep' and 'finish' with a clause could be a continuative or a completive on the verb as 'begin' and 'stop' are, and an inceptive or cessative in a command, as in 'stop talking!', or on be, as in 'stop being a fool', which keep their verbs today; the odds of inflecting for perfective and imperfective, a guess of 30% where WALS 65A counts 101 of 222; and a perfect, progressive or habitual in a deranked clause, which loses it along with its tense. A perfect could also lean on what the language already has, as one from 'have' wants a verb for owning and a language whose possession is 'at me is' would make one from the copula, and split ergativity, as Hindi is ergative only in the perfective, waits on aspect too.

## Noun phrases

- **More demonstratives.** Third person pronouns made from demonstratives, which WALS 43A finds in more than half of 225 languages, as Latin *ille* became French *il*, related to all of them, or only the far one, or only for things; case on a demonstrative beside its noun, as Latin *hunc* and Russian *ètogo* have; demonstratives on both sides of the noun, and with none of them dominant, which WALS 88A counts in 84 languages; four or more distances, which a clause file can't say; demonstratives that say more than distance, as some languages tell apart uphill and downhill or seen and unseen; and 'such' and 'so', the demonstratives of kind and degree.
- **More quantifiers and indefinites.** A quantifier away from its noun or on a pronoun, as in *they are all smiling* and *what we all want*, which the glosser now drops; a series for *any* of its own, as Haspelmath's semantic map tells apart the free choice of *anyone can come* from the *anyone* of questions and negation, which today becomes *someone* or *nobody*; *somehow*, whose manner is an adverb here and not a noun phrase, and *sometimes* and *once*, which say how often; quantifiers that agree with their noun, as Spanish *todos* and *todas* do; *both*, *several*, *other* and *enough*; distributives, as in *one to each*, which WALS 54A counts; and the languages left out today, the 2 of WALS 46A that say 'someone' with an existential construction and the 12 of 115A that say 'nobody came' as 'there is no one who came'.
- **Nouns modifying nouns.** `(key small iron)` and `(bow short yew)` pass a noun off as an adjective, and the parser can't tell. These need a form of their own, a compound, a genitive or a derived adjective, as each language prefers.
- **Measure phrases.** *A bowl of stew*, by a genitive or plain apposition, and numeral classifiers in the languages that have them.
- **Particles of focus.** *Only*, *just*, *even*.
- **Direction and result on verbs.** *Walk down*, *turn around*, *hold up*, whose particles are dropped today, and the result a particle can say, as *bite through* and *break off* do. English and Russian, whose *pere-kusit'* is 'across-bite', put these beside the verb, Mandarin compounds a result verb onto it, as in *yǎo-duàn* 'bite-break', and Spanish leaves them to the verb itself, which is how Talmy divides languages.

## Game text

- **Proper names.** *Marta* gets coined as a root like any word, and *Hollow* gets dropped. A name should come through recognizably, fitted to the language's sounds, and inflect or not as the language's names do.
- **Answering.** *Yes* and *no* are words of their own in every language, as in English. Sadock and Zwicky find two other systems: answers that agree or disagree with how the question was put, as Japanese *hai* answers a negative question, and answers that repeat its verb, as in Welsh and Finnish. Both need to know the question answered, which a fragment doesn't. Many languages also say 'no' with their negator, as Spanish *no* is both, where English tells *no* from *not*.
- **Insubordination.** A subordinate clause standing alone, as *If you come back.* follows *Buy me a drink when you come back*, which is refused today. It's mostly an afterthought joined to the clause before it, and in some languages a construction of its own, as a wish or a warning, which Evans calls insubordination.
- **More fragments.** A fragment that asks, as 'where?' and 'what?' do, and punctuation besides a heading's colon, which nothing in the output has.
- **Reference.** A pronoun's gender comes from the English, so 'it' stays neuter for a noun that's feminine in the language. Knowing what a pronoun refers to would fix that.

## Grammar in general

- **Inflection classes and irregularity.** Declensions and conjugations, stem changes, and irregular common words (*went*, *better*), which do as much as anything to make a language feel lived in.
- **Agreement.** Absolutive agreement, object agreement and differential object marking.
- **More verb categories.** Evidentiality and more moods.
- **Inalienable possession.** Separate marking for body parts and kin, as in many languages.
- **Politeness.** Formal 'you' and registers, for games whose characters have rank.

## Words

- **Word frequency.** A bundled lemma frequency table (with English length as the fallback for words it lacks), so frequent words come out shorter, per Zipf's law of abbreviation.
- **Derivation and compounding,** for words like *teacher* or *sunflower*, starting with agent nouns, which hollow.txt is full of (*hunter*, *miller*, *ferryman*), then participles (*broken*, *healing*), and motion nouns, the pairs like Latin *actor* and *actrix* or Spanish *panadero* and *panadera* that name a man or a woman with what are effectively two words, which may fall in different declensions. Checks against accidental homophones are harder than they sound: avoiding one would make a root depend on the words coined before it.
- **Colexification.** Every English word gets a root of its own, which makes every language a calque of English. Real languages divide meaning differently, with one word for 'hand' and 'arm', or 'blue' and 'green', in WALS's proportions.

## Sounds

- **More of free stress.** The first accent in a word always wins, as in Russian; other languages let the last win, or have dominant affixes that override the root's accent, as English *-ity* moves the stress of *eléctric* to *electrícity*. Spelling always marks stress that breaks the rule, though Russian marks none and Italian only a final one (*città*). Secondary stress, too, if anything needs it.
- **More of clitics.** Particles lean on the word beside them and are said with it, but adpositions and conjunctions are roots, so they're stressed and stand alone, though they're often clitics in real languages. Clitics are always written apart, where Spanish joins its enclitics (*dígamelo*) and French elides with an apostrophe (*l'ami*), and nothing goes in second place but a question particle, as Wackernagel's clitics do in many languages.
- **More diphthongs.** Falling diphthongs are single vowels that end on *i* or *u*. Rising ones (*ia*, *ua*) are a glide and a vowel, which is fine for most languages, and there are no triphthongs or diphthongs ending on other vowels, like English *ɪə*. Diphthongs only ever come from the inventory or from vowels meeting; breaking, as Spanish *e* became *ie* under the stress (*tierra*), and monophthongization under the stress too, would let one language's diphthongs come from another's vowels.
- **Inventory sizes against real ones.** Inventories should have the size spread real ones have when counted over the sounds generated here, which PHOIBLE's inventories can check.
- **More sound changes.** Allophones of the kinds still missing: palatalized consonants before front vowels, nasalized vowels before nasals, and dark *l*. Across words, only the changes that look at a sound's neighbors apply, not those that need syllables, like Andalusian *s* weakening before a consonant of the next word, and liaison happens before any vowel in the sentence, where French makes it only between words that belong together, as an article and its noun.

## Beyond one language

- **Language families.** Daughter languages that descend from a shared ancestor by regular sound change and grammatical drift, with the cognates that gives them, for a generated world's history. The ordered sound changes are a start on this.
- **Borrowing.** Loanwords adapted to the sounds of the borrowing language, between languages in contact.
- **Writing systems.** Generated scripts, alphabets, syllabaries and logographies, which is half of what a game like Chants of Sennaar is.
- **Place and personal names.** Built from the lexicon, as in Dwarf Fortress, so that a village's name means something in the language it's named in.

## Configuring generation

A spec fixes some of a language's settings and draws the rest, as [spec-format.md](spec-format.md) describes, and [configuration.md](configuration.md) records how it works. Every stage that makes a decision with WALS behind it has settings. What's left:

- **A frontend.** A console app is unwieldy for this beyond the spec file. Blazor, run locally, would share the C# types and build its forms from the declared settings, and generation at about 32 ms makes a live preview easy. It could move to WebAssembly later for a public site, though its speed there is unmeasured. Settings would be grouped on the left and the language on the right, with its paradigms and sample glossed sentences, and an inventory would be a clickable IPA chart with each sound required, forbidden or free, in an order that can be rearranged, since the first sound is the commonest. Very small inventories want a warning: a typical language has 10 to 15 homophone pairs among 250 roots, a stops-only one 32, and a glides-only one 110. The main workflow would be to generate, lock what you like and reroll the rest, and the rules would grey out what's no longer possible, and say why.
- **One description of a language.** The command line and a frontend should render one structured description rather than each having their own, which means splitting `LanguageDescription`, which only writes text today.
- **Settings for the rest.** A few stages decide on guessed odds with no settings: manner adverbs, how duration is said, and the intensifier. Inside stages that have settings, some details have none: whether the noun stays singular after a numeral and how compound numerals are ordered, articles agreeing in number and their side of the noun, a neuter among gendered pronouns, verbs agreeing in gender, the side ordinal and imperative markers go on, and which role's marker a deranked clause takes. The spelling style would want a setting for each of its dozen choices of letters. A setting for how many cases, for "lots of them, whichever", and an override for a region a semantic map can't draw could come if someone wants them.
- **Settings for what's to come.** Everything on this roadmap will want settings of its own, and some of it will rework existing ones, since settings mirror the shape of what they control: free stress, inflection classes and more agreement would each change them. A spec that no longer fits should fail with a clear error rather than be reinterpreted.
- **More tests that fix what they need.** Most tests that took a language with some feature from the shared sample now fix it with a spec; the filters left are on features with no setting, which move as they get one, or are tests that something holds in every language with a feature, which could run over a few spec-fixed seeds instead.
- **Random specs with shrinking.** The random-spec test is already a property test: any spec is refused or met. What it lacks a library would give, CsCheck being the one to weigh first, since it's built for C# and shrinks from its generators, against FsCheck, which is F# first and wants shrinkers of its own.
  - *Shrinking.* A failure is reported as the whole spec, some 32 settings, of which a few matter; the subordinator bug it found came down by hand to four. A failure does report its spec as JSON and its seed, which reproduce it from the command line. A shrinker would drop fixed settings one at a time and widen a setting's values back toward free, keeping each step that still fails. Each step generates a language, at about 400 ms, so shrinking takes a minute or so, which is fine once something has failed.
  - *A generator over the declarations.* As `RandomSpec` does now, the generator would walk `Settings.All` and draw from each setting's declared values, inventories as an exact list or a few sounds required or forbidden, so new settings are covered without touching it. That's why this needn't wait for the features to settle: settings are added every PR, but how they're declared doesn't change.
  - *Two ways to run.* The usual suite keeps a fixed seed and a fixed number of specs, so it stays deterministic and about as long as now. An exploratory run, opted into by an environment variable or a test trait, draws fresh seeds and more specs. A failure either finds gets shrunk and kept as a fixed regression test, as the subordinator one was.
  - *Distribution, reported.* The library's classification would report how specs fall, refused and met and how many settings each fixes, in place of the bounds the test asserts, which drifted until the share of settings fixed had to drop from a quarter to a fifth.
  - *Calibration, reported too.* Whether drawn features follow WALS belongs in a report beside that one, not in tests. Tests used to draw a stage thousands of times on a fixed seed and assert shares in ranges picked by eye around the very table the stage draws from: they restated it, their ranges had no statistical basis, and the seed kept them from ever failing by chance. They're gone. A report would draw many languages and set each feature's share beside its WALS count, crossed with whatever its odds depend on, like word order, alignment or fusion, since handing a stage the wrong condition, OV for VO, is the one mistake those tests caught that nothing else does. It would also check the floor `SettingWrites` relies on: every value it can't write in must be drawn at least 2% of the time under every condition its odds depend on, and nothing checks that now. The few tests that still check a share, of a sampler or of something no table sets, like how often roots collide, take their bounds from a binomial at a stated false-failure rate.
  - *Then clauses.* Generating random statements waits until the clause model settles, since it grows each PR. Its prerequisite is a printer from `Statement` to clause-file text, which would make properties of the parser and realizer possible: reading back what was printed gives the same statement, anything the parser accepts realizes, and a seed always gives the same language.
- **Stages as a graph, later.** Once every stage's result has a type of its own and every deciding stage is split from the words it coins, a stage's signature lists its dependencies, and for a decision those are also the dependencies of its odds. `Generate` could then declare its stages and their inputs by type, as constructor injection does, and run them in an order worked out from the graph, with groups derived as a fixed setting's deciding ancestors instead of listed. An attempt would be a child scope: its group's stages are rebuilt with that attempt's randoms, everything outside it is shared, and an abandoned attempt is discarded. It would still be Pure DI, composed in one place, rather than a container: each stage has only one implementation, and nothing in DI covers rebuilding a scope until it meets a condition. It becomes worth building when there are more groups than are easy to keep straight by hand, or when tests want to swap in parts of a language. Until then, giving stage results types of their own as they're touched moves toward it at no cost.
