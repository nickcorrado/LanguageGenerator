# Roadmap

[examples/hollow.txt](../examples/hollow.txt), a short text adventure, is the test of what real text needs. The front end refuses 23 of its 190 sentences. Refusals are the easy part, though: a refusal names what's missing, while a sentence that glosses can still lose half of what it said. *They pass so close that you could touch them* comes out as just 'they pass very close', and *at dusk* doesn't come out at all. So this list counts both.

## Clauses

Roughly in the order to build them, since the later ones lean on the earlier ones.

- **More adverbs.** Words for where (*here*, *there*, *upstream*), which come with demonstratives; *ago* and *later* after a noun (*three winters ago*, *a month later*); manners that compare (*more slowly*); and a time first in the clause, as many languages put one, where today every adverb goes with the obliques, ranked by how near the verb it goes.
- **Places in noun phrases.** A noun phrase should be able to take a place, for *the old woman from the gate* and *a hunter at the fire*, which today only a clause can.
- **Roles English says with places.** English borrows spatial words for meanings that aren't places, and a language should borrow them only as its own map of roles says, as a time already shares the location's marker in most languages and not all. The ones the glosser now has to drop or bend:
  - Manner as a noun phrase (*in a line*, *in silence*). Russian puts it in the instrumental, *idti gus'kom* 'walk goose-INS' for 'in single file', Latin in the ablative, and others in an essive or a converb ('having become a line'), so it wants a marker with edges to the instrument, companion and location.
  - Opposition (*against the wind*, *fight against*), which German says with *gegen*, apart from *an* for touching. It takes a facing word or the goal's marker in many languages. The `against` configuration should then mean touching alone.
  - Having or wearing (*in armor*, *a man with a beard*), mostly marked like a companion, sometimes like a place, as Russian *v dospekhakh* is, and by a case of its own in Australian languages, like Warlpiri's proprietive *-kurlu*.
- **Existentials.** 'There is a dog in the house', which many languages say unlike 'the dog is in the house'.
- **More modals.** Epistemic modals (*might*, *must be*, *maybe*), which often share markers with the situational ones that are there now, and which apply to a clause's tense rather than taking it, as *he must have left* is certain now about a past. A modal on a command or a comparison. Languages whose negation is an affix could show its scope by where it goes, as Turkish *git-me-meli* 'must not go' puts it inside the modal and *gid-eme-z* 'cannot go' outside, and some languages put a modal's verb in a subjunctive, as Greek and the Balkan languages do, where today it's always bare.
- **More questions.** *When*, *how*, *which* and *whose*; *who are you?*, with a question word as what *be* says; more than one question word, as in *who saw what?*; questions inside other clauses (*did anyone see what happened?*), which come with complement clauses; alternative questions with *or*; and a yes-no marker in content questions too, as Japanese *ka* is, which WALS doesn't count. Question words are often built on one another, as Turkish *ne* 'what' is in *nere* 'where', and often double as indefinites, as Mandarin *shénme* is both 'what' and 'something'.
- **More coordination.** Where the conjunction goes, which WALS doesn't count and is always between the two things here, though Latin *-que* follows the second; 'both … and' and 'either … or'; dropping a repeated subject (*she turns and walks away*); and coordinated addressees (*Marta and Ann, come*).
- **More subordinate clauses.** Deranked copular clauses; *although*; the subject of a deranked clause in the genitive, as many languages put it; and a nominalizer on a deranked verb that takes a case, where the language has one.
- **Complement clauses and reported speech.** *I told you that you were late*, *she wants to go home*. Today *"You're late," she says* glosses as a separate clause, *she says*, that doesn't say what she said.
- **Relative clauses.** *Nobody who goes into the forest*, *a clearing where nothing grows*, by a relativizer, a gap or a resumptive pronoun, before or after the noun as WALS has them.
- **More comparison.** Comparatives of adverbs (*slower than before*), inside a noun phrase (*stronger arms than yours*, *a bigger dog*) or with a clause as the standard (*warmer than it should*); a negated comparison or one with a modal; equatives (*as tall as*), which often share a marker with 'like'; similes with *be* (*she is like her mother*) or a clause (*like old men sharing a secret*); and superlatives. Comparing with *less*, too, which many languages can only say by negating *more*.
- **More voice.** Antipassives, which ergative languages favor (WALS 108A), starting with the kind that demotes an indefinite object; reflexives; and causatives.
- **More tense and aspect.** A perfect (*has fallen*, *have gone missing*), which is glossed as a plain past today, a progressive, and phase verbs like *begin to*.

## Noun phrases

- **Demonstratives.** *This village* loses its *this*, and a bare *this* or *that* becomes 'it'.
- **Quantifiers and indefinites.** *All*, *each*, *most*, *few*, *someone*, *everything*, and above all *nobody*, which can only be 'anyone' with a negated verb, a word the language coins as if it were a noun. Languages differ in whether a negative indefinite takes ordinary negation too.
- **Nouns modifying nouns.** `(key small iron)` and `(bow short yew)` pass a noun off as an adjective, and the parser can't tell. These need a form of their own, a compound, a genitive or a derived adjective, as each language prefers.
- **Measure phrases.** *A bowl of stew*, by a genitive or plain apposition, and numeral classifiers in the languages that have them.
- **Particles of focus.** *Only*, *just*, *even*.
- **Direction and result on verbs.** *Walk down*, *turn around*, *hold up*, whose particles are dropped today, and the result a particle can say, as *bite through* and *break off* do. English and Russian, whose *pere-kusit'* is 'across-bite', put these beside the verb, Mandarin compounds a result verb onto it, as in *yǎo-duàn* 'bite-break', and Spanish leaves them to the verb itself, which is how Talmy divides languages.

## Game text

- **Proper names.** *Marta* gets coined as a root like any word, and *Hollow* gets dropped. A name should come through recognizably, fitted to the language's sounds, and inflect or not as the language's names do.
- **Fragments.** Game text is full of headings and item names (*Item: Rusty Key*, *New quest: The Missing Children*), which need a form of their own for a bare noun phrase in place of an invented *the item is*.
- **Interjections and bare vocatives.** *No*, *hello*, and a vocative on its own, like *Stranger!*, which has no clause to go with.
- **Reference.** A pronoun's gender comes from the English, so 'it' stays neuter for a noun that's feminine in the language. Knowing what a pronoun refers to would fix that.

## Grammar in general

- **Inflection classes and irregularity.** Declensions and conjugations, stem changes, and irregular common words (*went*, *better*), which do as much as anything to make a language feel lived in.
- **Agreement.** Absolutive agreement, object agreement and differential object marking.
- **More verb categories.** Evidentiality and more moods.
- **Inalienable possession.** Separate marking for body parts and kin, as in many languages.
- **Politeness.** Formal 'you' and registers, for games whose characters have rank.

## Words

- **Word frequency.** A bundled lemma frequency table (with English length as the fallback for words it lacks), so frequent words come out shorter, per Zipf's law of abbreviation.
- **Derivation and compounding,** for words like *teacher* or *sunflower*, starting with agent nouns, which hollow.txt is full of (*hunter*, *miller*, *ferryman*), then participles (*broken*, *healing*), and motion nouns, the pairs like Latin *actor* and *actrix* or Spanish *panadero* and *panadera* that name a man or a woman with what are effectively two words, which may fall in different declensions. Checks against accidental homophones are harder than they sound: avoiding one would make a root depend on the words coined before it.
- **Colexification.** Every English word gets a root of its own, which makes every language a calque of English. Real languages divide meaning differently, with one word for 'hand' and 'arm', or 'blue' and 'green', in WALS's proportions.

## Sounds

- **Free stress,** which each word stores, as in Russian or Spanish, with accents for orthographies to mark it where it's unpredictable, as Spanish marks *teléfono*. It's harder to learn than the predictable stress there is today. Secondary stress, too, if anything needs it.
- **Diphthongs.** Vowels are single segments today, long or short. A vowel followed by a glide in its coda already sounds like one (*aj*, *aw*) but isn't written or treated as one, and the close vowels Greek contraction leaves beside other vowels are where Greek made its diphthongs.
- **Inventory sizes against real ones.** Inventories should have the size spread real ones have when counted over the sounds generated here, which PHOIBLE's inventories can check.
- **More sound changes.** Allophones that aren't phonemes of the language, and changes across word boundaries, like French liaison.

## Beyond one language

- **Language families.** Daughter languages that descend from a shared ancestor by regular sound change and grammatical drift, with the cognates that gives them, for a generated world's history. The ordered sound changes are a start on this.
- **Borrowing.** Loanwords adapted to the sounds of the borrowing language, between languages in contact.
- **Writing systems.** Generated scripts, alphabets, syllabaries and logographies, which is half of what a game like Chants of Sennaar is.
- **Place and personal names.** Built from the lexicon, as in Dwarf Fortress, so that a village's name means something in the language it's named in.

## Configuring generation

Fixing some of a language's properties before it's generated, like its alignment, its word order or its phonemic inventory, and drawing the rest as usual. The first phase and phase 2a are built, as [spec-format.md](spec-format.md) describes: the spec, exact inventories, the settings of phonology, word order, the morphological profile and alignment, and, drawn together with the last three, the settings of role regions, cases and how configurations are marked, with the rules that keep them coherent. The settings of negation, polar questions, the passive, modals, possession, genders and comparison are built too, as single stages that rules keep coherent with the morphology, and so is phase 2b: the settings of sound changes, and inventories given as the sounds they require and forbid, and how many they have. The rest of this records the design of what's built and the plan for what follows. Before any of it, a patch to a throwaway copy, which fixed vowels, consonants, syllable template, word order, fusion and alignment in about seven lines, suggests the generator suits it well:

- 3,024 languages covering every combination of word order, alignment, fusion and syllable template rendered all of hollow.txt's clauses and every paradigm without an error.
- 500 random inventories and 13 hand-picked extreme ones, like stops only or a single consonant and vowel, gave no errors and no sounds outside the inventory. Only an empty vowel or consonant list crashed.
- Fixing properties to the values a seed already gave reproduced the same language 300 times out of 300.
- Changing alignment or word order left every root the same in 200 of 200 languages. Changing the vowels changed the roots in all 200.

Those runs show that the pipeline holds together, not that every combination reads well.

- **The spec.** `Generate` would take a `LanguageSpec` beside the seed, with each setting either free or one of a set of values. That's about 45 single-choice settings and 15 structured ones. Inventories would be IPA strings and everything else enum names, so a spec is a small JSON file, and `--spec file.json` gets the feature to the command line before there's any frontend. A blank field means free, so a spec written for an older generator still fits a newer one with more settings, and a field the generator doesn't know is an error. A spec can also be read off a finished language, for any of its settings, which is how a frontend would lock what you like and reroll the rest.
- **Fixing a value by redrawing.** Most settings need no change to their stage: it's drawn again until the result matches, which keeps the conditional odds right (fixing SOV still draws adposition order from the OV counts) and leaves a seed's language alone when its first draw already matches. Configuration is partly for languages the odds would never give, so a value no draw reaches is set directly: when no draw within a generous cap matches, the draw that matched the most fixed settings has the rest written in. Nothing has to declare which values are unreachable, which would drift from the odds. A value set directly can disagree with a free one drawn beside it in the same stage, which a stage only needs to handle, by drawing the rest given the fixed value, once some setting runs into it.
- **Conflicts between stages.** This is the real design work. Generation only runs forward, and it often overrides a value without saying so: isolating morphology turns prefixed negation into a particle and head-marked possession into dependent marking, and takes away gender markers; syllable contact and coda sound changes need a template with codas; verbal predicative adjectives can't have adjective agreement; vowel harmony and unstressed reduction need particular vowels. A table of perhaps 10 to 20 rules handles these. A rule is an implication from a later setting to a condition on an earlier stage's result: "prefixed negation requires not isolating", or "a dative case requires the recipient to head its region". Most conditions just narrow another setting's values, which is the same kind of check `Setting.Matches` already makes. A condition can also depend on what earlier stages in its group drew, for a spec that allows either of two values only one of which an earlier stage can permit: on said like at or by a case is joined to at only where the language can't have spatial cases. Inside a group, an attempt that misses a condition is just rejected, as one that misses a setting is, which keeps the odds exact. Before anything is drawn, the generator closes the spec under the rules, so each earlier stage gets the conditions its later fixed settings imply, and the rules are used three ways:
  - **Refusing.** A spec is refused, with the rule's reason, when the conditions leave a setting no values or contradict what's fixed, like fixed edges that already join the recipient to the possessor. The current rules, a coda restriction with a codaless template and VO with obliques between object and verb, become cases of this.
  - **Keeping write-ins coherent.** Where a value has to be written in, the stages before it were drawn to meet its conditions, so a spatial case is never written into an isolating language.
  - **Greying out.** A frontend computes the same conditions to show what's no longer possible, and why.

  A rule that holds of every language the generator makes changes nothing inside a group of stages drawn together (below): every attempt that matches already meets its condition, so the group draws the same languages. It only matters where exact odds are already lost. Outside a group it gives coherence without exact odds: fixing prefixed negation keeps morphology from being isolating, but doesn't make heavy prefixing likelier. So rules give coherence, and a stage joins a group only when its odds should be exact too. Every language in the free sample must meet every rule, which tests the rules against the generator, and every language the random-spec test makes must meet them too, which tests the write-ins. Only real contradictions are rejected, like a coda restriction with a template that has no codas: a setting that merely does nothing, like affixation in an isolating language, is accepted, or a spec read off a real language would fail. Where a setting can be shaped so a contradiction can't be written, that's better than a rule: core cases always follow the alignment, so they aren't settings.
- **Drawing related stages together.** Some settings depend on earlier stages: which cases a language has depends on its alignment and role regions, and how it marks configurations depends on its morphological profile, word order and cases. Redrawing one stage alone keeps the earlier ones as they fell, so a spec fixing a spatial case in a language that came out isolating could only ever write it in. Stages like these are drawn as a group instead. `Generate` wraps the lines that belong together in one `DrawTogether` call, and the group is drawn again until all of its fixed settings match. That's rejection sampling across stages, so every free setting in the group has the right odds given the fixed ones, and fixing a spatial case makes concatenative morphology likelier, as it should.
  - **What an attempt redraws.** Attempt 0 draws each stage with the key it has today, so a seed that already matches is unchanged. Attempt n redraws the stages up to and including the last one with a fixed setting, each with "{stage} #{n}", and reruns the stages after it with their attempt 0 randoms on the new inputs. A stage after the last fixed one can't change whether the attempt matches, so the odds stay exact, and more of the seed's language survives: fixing ergative redraws only the alignment, keeping the seed's regions. This needs only the order the stages are drawn in, not their dependencies.
  - **What matches.** Every fixed setting in the group, on every stage in it. A stage with fixed settings of its own isn't redrawn alone inside a group, and settings fixed on stages before the group are settled already.
  - **The cap and the fallback.** A group gets 10,000 attempts, each abandoned at the first stage that misses. If none matches, it falls back to drawing its stages one at a time, as stages outside groups are, each drawn up to 10,000 times to match given the stages before it and its fixed values written in if no draw reaches them. That writes in only what can't be reached given what came before, and the rules (above) make sure what came before allows it. Writing missing values into the closest attempt instead would leave later stages drawn for values that were then replaced.
  - **Which stages.** A group holds a fixed setting's deciding ancestors, the stages whose results change its odds. A stage is in one group at most, and groups with an ancestor in common merge, so in practice there's one: word order, morphology, alignment, role regions, cases and the configuration decisions. Fixing isolating then makes VO likelier, since morphology is drawn from the order of object and verb. A spec from phase one that fixed only word order or morphology, and wasn't met on the first draw, gives a different language after this, as a newer generator may. The groups are listed by hand, since there are only a few, and they're easy to check against the signatures.
  - **Deciding apart from coining.** A stage in a group decides without coining words, since an attempt may be thrown away and coining is the slow part. Configurations split into a decision, inside the group, and the adpositions' forms, coined after under their own key, "configuration words". That keeps the words the same whether or not the group needed redrawing. The stage coins today in the middle of its decisions, from the same random, so the split changes the configurations of free languages once, and only those: a free spec won't reproduce the previous version exactly, unlike in phase one, so the check is that nothing else changed. Role adpositions make no decisions, only a word for each region without a case, so they stay outside the group.
  - **Types.** The grouped stages' results get types of their own, and settings-like values passed to many stages, like the dropoff, become records, much as settings types are in DI, so that each signature says exactly what a stage depends on.
- **Case and configuration settings.** Each is a semantic map with markers, so they take the same shape:
  - **Cases.** A true or false setting for each oblique case, `genitive-case` through `perlative-case`, and `vocative-case`, so a spec can require a perlative and leave the rest free, or fix all nine for an exact set. Core cases follow the alignment and aren't settings. A setting for how many cases, for "lots of them, whichever", could come later.
  - **Role regions.** A true or false setting for each of the role map's 15 edges, like `roles-recipient-goal` for a recipient and goal sharing a marker. Edges are drawn independently, so a fixed edge is written in directly, without redrawing: the other edges keep their odds and the seed's other regions stay as they were. A region the map can't draw, like possessors and times alone, can't be written at all, since regions are the connected parts of the joined edges. That's the one place configuration doesn't allow the odd, because connectivity is what a region means here rather than a tendency, and allowing it would take a partition-shaped setting that fixes the whole partition and competes with the edges. It could come later as an override if someone wants it.
  - **Configuration regions.** A true or false setting for each of the configuration map's 7 edges, like `configurations-on-over`, the same way. An edge to At means saying a configuration as plainly as 'at'.
  - **Configuration markers.** A setting for each configuration but At, like `in-marker`, of `At`, `Adposition`, `RelationalNoun` or `Case`, though only a configuration the map joins to at by some path can be `At`, so under, behind, in front and around can't, saying how the configuration's region is marked, and `configuration-marking`, the preference most regions follow, so "a language with spatial cases" needs one setting. The preference isn't kept today, so the decision's result has to keep it for a spec to be read off a language. Which noun a relational noun is and which case a spatial adposition governs are left free for now. A relational noun written in may reuse a noun another configuration has, as 'face' says both in front and against in many languages.
  - **Their rules.** These are conditions on stage results, many of them about connectivity, which narrowing one setting couldn't say: a case needs its role to head its region, so a dative needs no chain of joined edges from the recipient to the possessor; `At` means the configuration's region holds At; configurations fixed to different markers can't share a region; and a spatial case needs fusion that isn't isolating and a case on the location's region, whichever role heads it. Each is refused up front when fixed edges already break it, and in the fallback the free edges are drawn again until it holds, which can always be done when the spec wasn't refused.
- **Stages as a graph, later.** Once every stage's result has a type of its own and every deciding stage is split from the words it coins, a stage's signature lists its dependencies, and for a decision those are also the dependencies of its odds. `Generate` could then declare its stages and their inputs by type, as constructor injection does, and run them in an order worked out from the graph, with groups derived as a fixed setting's deciding ancestors instead of listed. An attempt would be a child scope: its group's stages are rebuilt with that attempt's randoms, everything outside it is shared, and an abandoned attempt is discarded. A value set directly, like an inventory, is a registered instance rather than a factory, and so is a part of the language a test fixes. It would still be Pure DI, composed in one place, rather than a container: a container picks among implementations, and each stage has only one, and nothing in DI covers rebuilding a scope until it meets a condition. It becomes worth building when there are more groups than are easy to keep straight by hand, or when tests want to swap in parts of a language. Until then, giving stage results types of their own as they're touched moves toward it at no cost.
- **Inventories.** Exact inventories already work, apart from validating that each list has something in it. An inventory is a list of the phonemes' IPA symbols, and its order is meaningful, as in Mark Rosenfelder's generators: the first sound is the commonest, so whoever fixes an inventory also controls how often each sound turns up, and a frontend should show the list in order and let it be rearranged. How steeply frequency falls along the list, a fixed 0.15 today, is a setting too. A fixed inventory needn't follow the implications generated ones do, like z only with s, since configuration is partly for doing odd things. Very small ones need a warning: a typical language has 10 to 15 homophone pairs among 250 roots, a stops-only one 32, and a glides-only one 110. Inventories can only use the existing 53 phonemes. Partial inventories turned out simpler than expected: rather than a helper that consults the spec at each of the consonant builder's decisions, each sound has a true or false setting, as a map's edges do, and an inventory is redrawn until it matches, which keeps the odds of the free sounds exact. A spec writes them as `{ "require": [...], "forbid": [...] }` in the inventory's place. Where no draw matches, the closest has the rest written in, so a z required with s forbidden comes without s, which the odds would never give. Counts, this many vowels or so many consonants, are settings of the inventory's stage the same way, redrawn to match; a count no draw gives cuts the rarest sounds the spec and its sound changes can spare, or adds the commonest it allows.
- **Sound changes.** These turned out not to need a helper that consults the spec. Changes apply in an order the generator fixes, so a spec only says which a language has: a true or false setting for each change, and one setting for each set of changes a language has one of at most, like `vowel-harmony` or `hiatus-resolution`. They're redrawn like most settings, rather than written straight in as the edges of a map are, since a few aren't drawn independently: vowel coalescence always comes with glide formation. A change written in takes its place in the order. What a change needs from earlier stages is a rule: codas from the syllable template, or sounds from the inventory, which a free inventory is drawn again to have, and an exact one is refused without. Syllable contact became a setting too, since a contact repair needs it.
- **Predication, numerals and articles.** `predicative-adjectives`, `zero-copula`, `locational-copula` and `adjective-agreement`, `numeral-base`, `numerals-before-noun` and `ordinals`, and `definite-article`, `indefinite-article` and `article-gender-agreement` work the same way. A value that needs no forms, like a numeral's side of the noun or a locational copula taken away, is written in where no draw gives it; the rest are only drawn, and the rarest, ordinals with only a word for 'first', is still drawn 2% of the time. Verbal adjectives never agree in a draw, but nothing contradicts it, so fixing both writes the agreement in. An article that's an affix needs morphology that isn't isolating, and one agreeing in gender needs genders and an article that can agree, which rules draw the earlier stages for.
- **Coordination and subordination.** `and-is-with` and `clause-and` are settings of the conjunction stage, and the subordinator map takes the modality map's shape: an edge setting for each of its 6 edges, and for each of its 7 subordinators a setting for where its marker goes and whether its clause comes first, besides `purpose-balance`, `reason-balance` and `temporal-balance`, one for each WALS chapter, the last for 'when' and the clauses that follow it. Subordination now keeps the edges it joined, as modals do, so they can be read back. A marker written in keeps its form and moves its whole region, so even a suffix in a VO language, which 1 of WALS's 338 has, is written in; joined subordinators share a marker, so they can't be fixed in different places, and an isolating language has no suffixes. Which role's marker a deranked clause takes stays free, since its odds are guesses.
- **Commands.** `imperatives`, `prohibitive-uses-imperative` and `prohibitive-negator` are settings of the command stage. An imperative written in reuses the form of one the draw has, glossed for its new numbers, so even a plural-only imperative, which 2 of WALS's 548 languages have, is written in when no draw gives it; only a second imperative of its own, or a prohibitive negator where none was drawn, needs a draw. The spelling style isn't a setting: it's a dozen independent choices of letters, which would want a setting of its own each.
- **Inflection and pronouns.** What nouns and verbs inflect for, `noun-number`, `tense`, `aspect` and `verb-agreement`, and the pronouns' `first-person-plural`, `gendered-pronouns` and `drops-subjects`, are settings of the paradigm and pronoun stages, which are redrawn to match like negation. These stages coin their markers and forms as they decide, so a value is only ever drawn, never written in, apart from dropping subjects, which needs no forms; every value is drawn at least 4% of the time, so a draw always gives it. Nothing here contradicts anything else, so there are no rules: agreement only changes the odds of dropping subjects, and the paradigms aren't drawn with the stages before them, so fixing `tense` doesn't change how isolating a language is.
- **Keeping it up as the generator grows.** Everything on this list will want settings of its own, so each setting should be declared once, as a name, group, values and stage, with the spec, the JSON and the form controls all built from that list. Exposing a new feature is then a few declarations and maybe a conflict rule. A stage nobody exposes just stays random, so settings can lag behind the generator. The command line and a frontend should render one structured description rather than each having their own, which means splitting `LanguageDescription`, which only writes text today. What can't be designed away is rework of existing stages, since settings mirror the shape of what they control: free stress, diphthongs, inflection classes and more agreement would each change settings. A spec that no longer fits should fail with a clear error rather than be reinterpreted.
- **A frontend.** A console app would be unwieldy for this beyond the spec file. Blazor, run locally, would share the C# types and build its forms from the declared settings, and generation at about 32 ms makes a live preview easy. It could move to WebAssembly later for a public site, though its speed there is unmeasured. Settings would be grouped on the left and the language on the right, with its paradigms and sample glossed sentences, and an inventory would be a clickable IPA chart with each sound required, forbidden or free. The main workflow would be to generate, lock what you like and reroll the rest, which independent stages make nearly free.
- **Order to build it.** The spec, the declared settings, exact inventories and the settings of the stages the morphosyntax above won't reshape (phonology, word order, morphological profile and alignment), with the rules they need, the random-spec test and `--spec`, first, at a rough guess about a week. Then, in phase 2a, drawing related stages together, with case sets and configurations as its first users and the rules for what's written in when a group runs out of draws. In 2b, the stages that consult the spec as they draw, for partial inventories and sound changes. The simpler morphosyntax settings, like negation and possession, can go with either. That's about another week. The frontend, one to two weeks, should wait until the morphosyntax above settles, or be only a thin generated form until then.
- **Tests that fix what they need.** Once the spec work is done, tests about languages with some feature can fix it with a spec instead of filtering the shared sample of 300 for it, 86 of them today, 38 guarding against finding none, or relying on what one seed happens to have. A filter can come up empty or thin whenever a change shifts the draws, and a spec can't, while its language is still a real one rather than a mock that could never be generated. Each such test should fix only what it's about and run over several seeds, so the rest still varies, and the specs should be shared and generated once, as the sample is. Tests that something holds in every language, and tests of how often something happens, stay on the free sample, since a spec skews the odds by design. So does anything about a value no draw reaches, which a spec writes in directly and which no ordinary language has. Only the filters on settings can move, 16 so far, so the rest follow as their features get settings, which is a reason to declare settings even for what few people would fix.
- **What it leaves out.** Fixing a later property reshapes only the earlier stages drawn in a group with it, so a property outside any group leaves the stages before it as they fell. A seed and a spec reproduce a language only on the same version of the generator, so saving a finished language for good would be a separate feature, as would writing your own roots and affixes.
