# Grammar

How a language orders and marks its words, from the shape of its phrases to the clauses it can build. Each part draws on the [phonology](phonology.md) for the forms of its words, and `Syntax.Realize` uses all of it to turn a clause into words.

## Word order

Constituent order, the head directionality of each phrase type, and where obliques go: almost always after the verb and object in VO languages, and before them, between them or after them in OV ones, in WALS's proportions.

## Morphology

How fusional or isolating the language is, whether it prefixes or suffixes, which category values are zero-marked, and paradigms of exponents for nouns and verbs.

## Alignment and cases

Nominative–accusative, ergative–absolutive or neither, in WALS's proportions, which cases exist, and whether each semantic role is marked by a case, an adposition or nothing.

The oblique roles sit on a semantic map, a graph whose edges are the ways languages mark two roles alike: recipients with goals, as English *to* does, goals with locations, as Japanese *ni* does, possessors with sources, as Romance *de* does, companions with instruments, as English *with* does in about a quarter of languages, beneficiaries with recipients, as the Latin dative does, and a few more. Each language divides the map into connected regions, one marker apiece, so a role without a marker of its own takes its neighbors'.

How many regions get a case follows WALS's counts of case inventories, favoring the genitive, dative and locative as Blake's case hierarchy does, and the rest get an adposition, a short word of its own, before or after its noun as the language's adpositional phrases go.

## Noun phrases

### Gender

None, masculine and feminine, those and a neuter, or animate and inanimate, in WALS's proportions, assigned by meaning (people by sex, as a noun file says) and, in about half the languages with gender, by the root's last sound too. Agreeing adjectives, and in some languages verbs, take a marker for their noun's gender.

### Pronouns

One short form per person and number, with inclusive and exclusive 'we' in some languages, and none at all in others; masculine and feminine (and sometimes neuter) forms in the third person, or the second and third, in about a third of languages; case markers shared with nouns where nouns have a case slot of their own; and whether subject pronouns are usually dropped, leaving the verb's agreement to say who.

### Numerals

Decimal, vigesimal with tens below twenty (Basque *hogeita hamazazpi*, 'twenty and seventeen', is 37), or words for only the first few numbers and then 'many', in WALS's proportions, before or after the noun as WALS has them. Compound numbers mostly put the largest part first, and in some languages a noun after a numeral stays singular, as in Turkish *üç kurt* 'three wolf'.

Ordinals follow WALS's types too: most languages have a word of their own for 'first', some for 'second' as well, and build the rest from cardinals with a marker on the whole numeral (*thirty-seven-th*), a prefix, a suffix or, in isolating languages, a word, as Mandarin puts *dì* before *sān* for 'third'; others build every ordinal, and some just use the cardinal.

### Possessors

Marked on the possessor with a genitive case or an adposition, on the possessed noun with an affix agreeing with the possessor (Hungarian *a király ház-a* 'the king house-3SG'), on both (Turkish *kral-ın ev-i*), or on neither, in WALS's proportions. Possessors go where the language's adpositions put their objects, per Greenberg's Universal 2, and where only the possessed noun is marked, the affix alone says 'my' or 'his'.

### Articles

A definite article, an indefinite one, both or neither, in WALS's proportions. Each is a word at the edge of the noun phrase or an affix on the noun, and an indefinite article is often the word for 'one', for one thing only, so 'dogs' stays bare.

In most languages with gender, articles agree with their noun's gender, and in some a definite article agrees in number too, as Spanish has *el*, *la*, *los* and *las*. In some languages the definite article is the demonstrative.

### Demonstratives

'This' and 'that', telling apart two distances, three, as Spanish *este*, *ese* and *aquel* do, or none, in WALS 41A's proportions, before or after the noun or, rarely, an affix on it, as WALS 88A counts them, agreeing with it as articles do.

Standing alone, a demonstrative is mostly the same word, as WALS 42A counts, and *here* and *there* are mostly the demonstratives marked as places, as Japanese *koko-ni* 'this place-LOC' is, or else words of their own. Some languages keep the definite article beside a demonstrative, as Greek *aftó to spíti* 'this the house' does.

### Quantifiers and indefinites

*All*, *every*, *some*, *many*, *few*, *most* and *no*, each a word beside its noun.

*Someone*, *nothing*, *everywhere*, *never* and the rest are made from question words, as Japanese *dare-ka* 'who-INDF' is, from nouns for 'person', 'thing', 'place' and 'time', as English *some-body* is, or from words of their own, in WALS 46A's proportions. In most languages *nobody* takes the verb's negation too, as Russian *nikto ne prišël* 'nobody not came' does, as WALS 115A counts them.

### Adjectives

Before or after the noun, as each language's noun phrases go, and either bare, agreeing with the noun in number and case, or with the whole phrase marked once at its edges.

## Verbs

### Negation

A word before or after the verb, a prefix or suffix on it, or a word on each side, like French *ne … pas*, in WALS's proportions, with isolating languages using words where others would use affixes.

### Commands

An imperative with its own form for singular and plural, for only one of them or for both alike, often a bare stem, or none at all, leaving the ordinary second person, in WALS's proportions. Prohibitives use the imperative or the second person, with ordinary negation or a negator of their own, like Latin *nē*.

### Passives

A passive marked on the verb, next to its root, in WALS's proportions for each alignment: most nominative-accusative languages have one, but few ergative ones, whose patients already take the intransitive subject's case.

The agent is a role on the [semantic map](#alignment-and-cases), marked like an instrument, a source or a place, or by a word of its own like English *by*. A language without a passive says the active, with the agent as subject, or 'they' where nobody says who.

### Perfect, progressive and habitual

'Has gone', 'is waiting' and 'used to go', each by an affix, a word of its own, or an auxiliary verb that takes the tense and agreement, as a [modal verb](#modals) does.

A perfect follows WALS 68A: none in about half of languages, which say it as a perfective past, and otherwise from 'have', as English does, from 'finish', as in much of Southeast Asia, or another way, as French *est venu* uses the copula.

A progressive is mostly the copula that says where, as Spanish *está esperando* is, or 'sit', as Dutch *zit te wachten* is, and where there's none it's an imperfective, as a habitual is in most languages.

Beginning and ceasing to do something are verbs, 'begin' and 'stop', in most languages, and an inceptive or cessative on the verb in some, as Russian *za-pel* 'started singing' is.

### Modals

'Can', 'may', 'must' and 'should', each by an affix on the verb, as Turkish *-ebil*, a word of its own, or a verb, as French *pouvoir*, in WALS 74A's proportions. A modal verb takes the tense and agreement and leaves the verb bare beside it, before it in VO languages and after it in OV ones, as auxiliaries go.

Ability and permission often share a marker, as English *can* does, and so do 'must' and 'should', as French *devoir* does, along a semantic map like the [subordinators'](#subordinate-clauses), and a shared marker is glossed by what it means in each use.

Epistemic 'might' and 'must', the speaker's judgment of the whole clause, as in 'he must have left', are an affix, a particle or an impersonal verb with the clause as its complement, as Greek *borí na éfige* 'it may that he left' is, in WALS 75A's proportions, and share the situational markers as often as WALS 76A counts.

## Clauses

### Copular clauses

A short copula, glossed COP, which some languages leave out in the present, and adjectives that either take it or inflect like verbs themselves, as in Mandarin *māo dà* 'the cat is big', each in WALS's proportions. Most languages say where something is with a verb of their own, glossed be.at, like Spanish *estar* beside *ser*, and the rest with the copula.

### Questions

Yes-no questions by a particle, first, last or after the first word, as Mandarin *ma* and Russian *li* go, by an affix on the verb, by putting the verb first, as German does, or by intonation alone, in WALS 116A's and 92A's proportions.

Questions with 'who', 'what', 'where', 'when', 'why' and 'how' put their question word first, as in English, or leave it in its place, as in Mandarin, in the share WALS 93A counts for each word order. A question word takes its role's case, as Turkish *nere-den* 'where from' does.

### Coordination

'And', 'or' and 'but' between noun phrases, adjectives and clauses. 'And' between nouns is the word for 'with' in nearly half of languages, as WALS 63A counts them, so 'the dog with the cat slept', and the same word joins clauses in some languages and not others, or none at all, as WALS 64A has it. Things joined by 'and' are plural to what agrees with them, or dual where the language has one, and 'you and I' is 'we'.

Events joined under one tense and sentence type, as in 'she turns and walks away', are each said as a clause at the shared tense, with a subject the one before shares left unsaid.

### Subordinate clauses

Clauses saying if, when, before, after, until, because and in order to, each marked by a word at the start of the clause, a word at its end or a suffix on its verb, as WALS 94A counts them by word order. In OV languages they nearly always come first; in VO languages conditionals do, as Greenberg's Universal 14 has it, and reason and purpose clauses mostly follow.

Some languages strip a subordinate verb of its tense and agreement, as WALS 125A to 127A count for purpose, 'when' and reason clauses, and leave out a subject the two clauses share.

Subordinators share markers along a semantic map of their own, as German *wenn* is both 'if' and 'when' and English *since* both 'after' and 'because', and a reduced clause can be marked like a role on the [role map](#alignment-and-cases), as a purpose clause is 'for drinking' with a dative or English *to drink* with its 'to'.

### Relative clauses

Before or after their noun, as WALS 96A counts them for each order of verb and object, at the edge of the noun phrase.

The noun's role in one is left empty, as Turkish *kitab-ı al-an öğrenci* 'the student who bought the book' leaves it, said by a relative pronoun at its start in that role's case, as German *der*, *den* and *dem* are, which is mostly the language's question word, or by an ordinary pronoun in its place, for subjects, objects and obliques each, in WALS 122A's and 123A's proportions and following Keenan and Comrie's Accessibility Hierarchy.

A relative clause is marked by a word beside its noun, as English *that* and Mandarin *de* are, by an affix that makes its verb a participle, as Turkish *-an* does, or by nothing.

### Complement clauses

Clauses as the objects of verbs, split as Noonan splits them by time reference: what's said, known or seen, with a time of its own, and what's wanted, tried or begun, whose time the main verb sets.

Each kind is marked by a complementizer at its start, as English *that*, at its end, as Japanese *to*, or as a suffix on its verb, or by nothing, as in Mandarin, and its verb may be deranked, as Turkish *git-mek* 'go-INF' is, and then marked like an object or a goal, as English *to go* is.

How 'want' is said follows WALS 124A: a verb whose complement leaves its subject implicit or expresses it, as Greek *thélo na páo* 'I want that I go' does, or an affix or a word on the verb of what's wanted, as Japanese *tabe-ta-i* 'eat-DESID-PRS' is 'wants to eat'.

A complement that keeps its verb goes last in its clause or where the object goes, as its complementizer leans, and what someone says word for word is a quotation, marked with the complementizer, as Japanese *to* is, with a word of its own, as Turkish *diye* 'saying', or not at all.

### Comparison

'Marta is taller than the men' as WALS 121A's types have it: with the standard marked like a place, mostly 'from', as in Turkish ('tall from the men'), with a particle like English *than*, as the object of a verb meaning 'exceed', as in Yoruba ('she is tall, exceeds the men'), or as two clauses, 'Marta is tall, the men are not tall'.

A comparison is a grade of any adjective, what *be* says, a noun's or a manner's, as the [degree model](degree-model.md) lays out, and the language's type says it wherever it is. In a noun phrase or a manner, the standard goes on the side the language puts standards, outside the noun and its adjectives, as English *stronger arms than yours* has it. Exceeding and two clauses are clause constructions, so a language comparing that way says a noun phrase's comparison as a relative clause, 'arms that are strong, exceed yours', or 'arms that are strong' with 'yours are not strong' after them, and a manner's with 'exceed' beside it or with a second clause denying it.

The standard can be a clause, as in 'warmer than it should be': balanced after a particle, as English and Russian *čem* have it, deranked in the standard's role, as Turkish *bekle-diğ-im-den* 'expect-NMLZ-1SG-ABL' is 'than I expected', or as the object of 'exceed', and in two clauses, 'it is warm, it should not be warm', where the language compares that way or can't derank the clause yet, as a copular one or one with a modal can't be. The clause leaves out its word where it's the comparison's, as comparative deletion has it, unless it's the verb.

Every language can say 'taller' with no standard, by a word like Turkish *daha* 'more', before or after the adjective as the language's adjective phrases go, which leans with its order of verb and object as WALS 83A and 91A count together, or an affix like English *-er*, and some mark the adjective beside a standard too, as English does and most languages of the other types don't. In a manner, the marker goes inside the one that makes the adverb. 'Much', as in 'much taller', is said with the language's 'very' for now.

Similes, 'it shines like water', take a word for 'like' or an affix on what something is like.

### Adverbs

Words of their own like 'now' and 'again'; manner adverbs made from adjectives, as the adjective itself, with an affix like English *-ly*, or with a word beside it like Mandarin *de*; times marked like places in most languages, as English *at night* and Turkish *gece-de* 'night-LOC' are; durations marked like an object, like a time or by a word for 'for'; and a word for 'very' on the same side as other degree words.

Adverbs and obliques go nearer the verb by what they say, manner nearest and time furthest, mirrored before the verb, and adjectives after the noun mirror the English order in most languages.

## Fragments

Lines with no verb, as game text is full of: a noun phrase on its own, as in 'an old iron key', in the case a noun is cited in; an oblique on its own, as in 'for your ears', marked as it is in a clause; a heading, as in 'Item: Rusty Key', its label and title each cited, with a pause between them written as a colon; an interjection, as in 'hello', a short root of its own, and 'yes' and 'no', words of their own as in English; and an addressee alone, as in 'stranger!', in the vocative where the language has one. Any of them but a heading can be said to someone, who comes first, as before a clause, but after an interjection or an answer.
